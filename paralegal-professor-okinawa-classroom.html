From Paralegal to Professor: An Unlikely Path from Okinawa to a Taichung Classroom

📅 February 12, 2026
✎ Mike McQuillan
⌚ 7 min read

Nobody plans to go from drafting legal motions in a Marine Corps office in Okinawa to running phonics drills with five-year-olds in Taichung. I certainly didn't. But the line between those two jobs, in retrospect, was a lot straighter than it looked at the time.

I'm Mike McQuillan — most people call me Mac — and people ask me, often, how an American kid from Florida ended up as a long-term educator in Taiwan. The short version is "I joined the Marines and never quite came home." The long version is more interesting, and it explains a lot about how I teach now.

The Marines were the door, not the destination

I enlisted at nineteen for the reasons most nineteen-year-olds enlist: I wanted structure, I wanted to be useful, and I wanted to be somewhere that wasn't where I grew up. The Marine Corps gave me all three within ninety days, which is faster than any other institution I've ever dealt with.

I was assigned as a paralegal — the Marine Corps' word for someone who supports the legal services side of the fleet. In practice that meant drafting documents, running case files, sitting through proceedings, and learning, very quickly, that a careless sentence in a legal context becomes someone else's bad day. Paralegal work is not glamorous. It is, however, extraordinary training in how to build something out of small, exact pieces. You don't get to be vague. The document either says what it means or it doesn't, and someone has to live with the difference.

Okinawa taught me what foreign actually feels like

I spent four years stationed in Okinawa. Most days were base. Most nights weren't. I learned just enough Japanese to be polite and not nearly enough to be fluent — a humbling state to live in for a few years. I ate things I couldn't pronounce, got lost in neighborhoods I had no business being in, and, slowly, learned the small art of being a guest in someone else's country.

That last part is the part that mattered. When I eventually moved to Taiwan and stepped into a classroom full of kids who didn't speak my first language, I already knew — in my body, not in theory — what it feels like to be the person who can't quite follow along. Every teacher I respect has had that experience somehow. You teach better when you remember being the slowest person in the room.

From Okinawa to Taichung — and the discovery that I could teach

I didn't leave the Marines planning to teach. I left planning to figure it out. A friend was in Taichung; he said come visit; I came; I stayed. Within a few months I was tutoring; within a year I was running classrooms; within three years I was writing lessons that the schools wanted to keep. The thing nobody tells you about teaching English in Asia is that the field rewards exactly the skills the Marines had been quietly drilling into me for four years: clarity under pressure, deep preparation, the patience to repeat something six different ways until it lands, and the discipline to plan a week ahead even when the day is on fire.

If you'd asked nineteen-year-old me whether I'd be in Taichung at thirty-five, designing curriculum for two schools and writing a 12-volume children's series anchored by a magical watch, I would have laughed. But the path makes sense from this end of it. The Marines didn't make me a teacher. They made me someone who could become one. There's a real difference, and it's the difference I want to write about more — both in the curriculum work and in the decision to write my own teaching materials rather than keep working with what was on the shelf.

What I tell people who are thinking about a path like this

A few things, if you're a young veteran or a young anyone wondering how to translate one improbable chapter into the next. First, the skills travel further than you think — especially the boring ones. Paying attention. Showing up. Writing things down. Second, "useful" is a better compass than "impressive"; usefulness will route you to the right work eventually, and impressiveness will route you to the wrong work for years. Third, you don't have to pick the destination yet. You only have to pick the next door, and you only have to pick it honestly.

I picked the Marines because I wanted structure. I picked Taiwan because I wanted to keep going. I picked the classroom because, when I tried it, I was good at it and I liked the work. I picked the books because the books I needed didn't exist. None of those decisions were grand. They were the small honest ones, in order, and they added up — for Mac McQuillan and for plenty of people I've met who took similarly winding routes — to a life I'd actually choose again.

Conclusion

From a paralegal's desk in Okinawa to a classroom in Beitun, Taichung is, on paper, an absurd career trajectory. In practice it is the most coherent thing I've ever done. The Marines taught me how to work; Okinawa taught me how to listen; Taiwan taught me how to teach; and the books taught me — are still teaching me — how to take everything underneath all of that and turn it into something a student can actually use. If there's a moral, it's small: pick the next door honestly. The path is the path.
