Behind the Books

Why I Wrote a 12-Book Series About a Magical Watch

📅 April 9, 2026
✎ Mike McQuillan
⌚ 9 min read

The pitch sounds like every kid's daydream: a teacher's watch can pull a class out of the room and into another time, place, or problem — and they have to learn their way home. The reason I wrote it isn't a daydream. It's a Tuesday-afternoon reading lesson that wasn't working.

My name is Mike McQuillan — Mac to most people — and I have spent more than a decade in Taiwanese classrooms, mostly in Taichung, mostly with kids between five and eighteen. Adventures in Mr. Mac's Class is a 12-volume storybook series, one volume per grade level from Grade 1 through Grade 12, paired with a 12-volume companion textbook series that turns each adventure into something a real teacher can actually run on a Monday morning. People ask me where the magical watch came from. The honest answer is that it came from a problem. The fun answer is that it came from a kid named Sammy who sat in the front row and did not, under any circumstances, want to read the assigned chapter.

The problem the books were trying to solve

A working teacher has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. You have to teach the curriculum — the standards, the language targets, the assessments — and you have to keep human beings interested in the curriculum for forty-five-minute blocks at a time, five days a week, ten months a year. The published storybooks I had access to were either fun and pedagogically thin, or pedagogically rigorous and not fun. I wanted both. The brief I gave myself was simple: a story that earns its place in the classroom and a classroom plan that earns its place in the story.

The magical watch is the device that makes both possible. Once you grant a class a watch that can drop them into Egypt during the construction of the pyramids, into a London laboratory in 1928, into a tide pool, into a printing press, the lesson and the adventure become the same thing. Mac McQuillan didn't invent that pattern — anybody who has read The Magic School Bus will recognize it. The difference is that this time the books were written by someone who still has to face thirty kids on Monday morning, so the adventures land where lessons actually need to land.

Twelve books because students grow up

The other reason I committed to twelve volumes is that students don't stop being students after one book. A second-grader needs short paragraphs, lots of dialogue, and a chapter she can finish before the bell. A tenth-grader needs subplots, ambiguity, and a teacher figure who is allowed to be wrong about something. If you write a single storybook for "kids," you've optimized for nobody.

By committing to one volume per grade, the series can grow up with the reader. The Grade 1 book is a picture-led adventure with controlled vocabulary; by Grade 12, the same characters are taking on tougher questions in tougher prose, and the watch has become less a gimmick and more a dramatic device. Same world, same teacher, same magical watch. Twelve different reading levels, twelve different conversational ranges, twelve different things a kid is ready to think about. The decision to write my own curriculum from scratch made the 12-grade arc obvious — once you're already designing per grade, you might as well let the story do the same.

Why the watch, specifically

A bus is unforgettable, but it's also taken. I needed a device that didn't cite Ms. Frizzle every time it left the page. A watch is small enough to be personal — a teacher carries it on his wrist; the kids notice it; it has a little ritual when it activates — and large enough, narratively, to do anything I need it to do. It's also a quiet metaphor: time is the thing every teacher most wishes they had more of, and giving the class a watch that bends time is a wink at the working teachers reading along.

Practically, the watch also makes the storybook and textbook integrate cleanly. Each storybook chapter ends in a destination; each textbook unit picks up that destination and turns it into a vocabulary set, a writing prompt, a discussion question, a project. The watch is the hinge between the two halves of the series, and it hands a teacher an honest answer to the question every student eventually asks: why are we learning this? Because today the watch took us here. So today this is the language we need.

Lessons from the road so far

A few things I've learned writing the series that I didn't expect. First, the textbook is harder than the storybook. Stories want to be told; lesson plans have to be tested. Second, kids forgive almost everything except condescension — write up to them, not down. Third, every volume gets one or two scenes I love that have to come out in editing because they don't serve the lesson. Those scenes go in a drawer and most of them end up powering a chapter in a future grade level, when the readers are old enough to handle them.

Fourth — and this is the lesson I keep relearning — the books work because they were written from a real classroom, not for one. The years I spent moving from a Marine paralegal's desk to a Taichung classroom taught me that the only honest curriculum is one that answers a real student's real confusion. Sammy, the kid in the front row, was the first beta reader. The questions he asked at the kitchen table at his mother's cram school in Beitun shaped half of what's now Volume 3.

Where the series is going

The series is being built one grade at a time, in tandem with the textbook, and is designed to be usable both as a classroom set and as a standalone read at home. Volumes are continuing to come out on a measured schedule — quality over speed — and the textbook companion is being kept genuinely teacher-ready, the kind of book a tired educator can pick up at 9:54 p.m. and use at 8:00 a.m. with confidence. If you're a teacher, a parent, or a school owner who'd like to know more, the contact page is the right next door — drop Mac a note and say what you're after.

Conclusion

The magical watch is a fun answer to a serious question: how do you write a series of children's books that doesn't insult the children, doesn't bore the teacher, and doesn't get in the way of actually learning English? You write twelve of them, you let the story age up with the reader, you give the teacher in the books a small object that bends time, and you build a textbook beside the storybook so the magic survives contact with Monday morning. That's the project. It is, in the most honest sense, the same project I've been working on since the first day I stood in front of a Taiwanese classroom and tried to make the lesson actually matter. The books are the long version. The watch is just where it starts.
