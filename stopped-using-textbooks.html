Why I Stopped Using Textbooks and Started Writing My Own

📅 March 19, 2026
✎ Mike McQuillan
⌚ 8 min read

For the first few years I taught in Taichung, I used the same textbooks every other school used. Then one Tuesday I realized I had been apologizing for them in three out of every five lessons — and I decided to stop.

I'm Mike McQuillan, and the headline of this piece sounds more dramatic than the actual decision was. I didn't burn the textbooks. I didn't write a manifesto. I just kept noticing, lesson after lesson, that I was rewriting the worksheets at home, skipping over the dialogues that didn't fit my students, and bringing in my own examples to make the units actually land. After a while it became obvious: I wasn't using the textbook. I was patching the textbook. So I cut the middleman.

Off-the-shelf curricula are written for everyone, which means they're written for nobody

There's nothing wrong with a published EFL textbook in the abstract. The problem is that "EFL student" is not a person. The five-year-old in Beitun whose parents speak Taiwanese at home is not the same learner as the eleven-year-old in Taipei whose mother already started English drills at three. A good textbook author has to aim for the average — and the average isn't sitting in any of my classrooms.

When Mac McQuillan taught with off-the-shelf books, the lessons that worked were the ones I had quietly customized; the lessons that bombed were the ones I'd left intact. That's not the textbook's fault. It's the format's fault. And once I started writing units to fit the actual students in the room, the failure rate dropped. Not to zero — teaching is a humble business — but enough that I never wanted to go back.

I wasn't using the textbook. I was patching the textbook. So I cut the middleman.

The hidden tax on teachers who use someone else's curriculum

There is a tax that nobody talks about, and it's paid in evenings. Every teacher who customizes a textbook is doing the curriculum design work twice — once when they read what's on the page, and again when they decide what to do instead. It looks like normal lesson prep, but it's not; it's invisible curriculum reconstruction, and over the course of a year it adds up to hundreds of unpaid hours.

When I shifted to writing my own materials, the upfront cost was higher — there's no way around the first year of building from scratch — but the per-week cost of teaching dropped sharply. I was no longer apologizing for a unit, no longer fixing it on the fly, no longer scrambling for a better example because the one in the book was about a place none of my students had ever heard of. The lessons I designed were already aimed at the kids in front of me, so the kids in front of me met them halfway.

What changed in the classroom — and what didn't

A few things changed quickly. Engagement went up because the units finally referenced things students actually cared about — night markets, scooters, the school cat — instead of imported scenes that asked them to imagine being someone they were not. Retention went up because I could spiral concepts deliberately across weeks instead of hoping the textbook's sequencing matched my class's pace. And — this surprised me — discipline issues went down. Bored students misbehave. Engaged students don't have time.

A few things didn't change. The kids who needed extra time still needed extra time. The kids who tested well still tested well. Writing my own materials didn't turn me into a different teacher — it just stopped me from undermining the teacher I already was. Curriculum is not magic; it is a tool. My Marine training in Okinawa is the part of me that insists on getting the tools right before blaming the user.

Five things I'd tell any teacher thinking about doing the same

First: don't start with a 12-grade arc. Start with one unit. Pick a unit you teach often and rewrite it from scratch — your own examples, your own sequencing, your own assessment. If it works better than the published version, you have your answer. If it doesn't, you have learned something equally valuable. Second: keep what works. There's no virtue in originality for its own sake. If a workbook page does the job, use it; the goal is the student's learning, not the author's ego.

Third: write for the student you actually have, not the student you wish you had. Fourth: take notes every week on what landed and what flopped — those notes become the second draft. Fifth: don't expect anyone to thank you for it. Custom curriculum is invisible labor; the only person who will notice the difference is the kid who suddenly understands a tense, and that kid won't connect the dot. They don't have to. You'll know.

Where this led — and is still leading

My first homemade unit became a binder. The binder became a folder of binders. Eventually the folders became Adventures in Mr. Mac's Class — a 12-volume textbook series that lines up grade by grade with a parallel storybook arc. The books are the formalized version of years of classroom rewrites. The story behind the storybook side of that series, and the magical watch at its center, belongs in its own piece — but the textbook side started here, on a Tuesday, with a worksheet I was tired of apologizing for.

Conclusion

Stopping the use of textbooks wasn't a rejection of textbooks. It was an honest reckoning with the fact that I was already not really using them — and that the work I was doing in the margins was the actual job. If you're a teacher reading this and you've been quietly rewriting the unit every Sunday night for the last three years, I have news: you're already a curriculum designer. The only question is whether you want to keep the work invisible or finally let it count.
