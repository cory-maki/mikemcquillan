Origin

From the shores of Florida

Mike McQuillan grew up on the Gulf side of Florida, where the weather is the curriculum — humid summers, hurricane seasons, the kind of childhood that teaches a kid to pay attention to the sky. He was the boy with a stack of library books and a permanent layer of sand on his sneakers. When friends asked what he wanted to do, he answered honestly: he wanted to see the world, and he wanted to be useful in it.

Both halves of that answer landed him, eventually, in a classroom 9,000 miles from home. But it took the Marine Corps to get him there.

Marine Corps

Four years as a Marine paralegal in Okinawa

At nineteen, McQuillan enlisted in the U.S. Marine Corps and was eventually stationed in Okinawa, Japan, where he served four years as a paralegal. For readers who don't know what a Marine paralegal does: it's a research-and-process job — drafting legal documents, supporting Judge Advocates on military justice cases, learning to write with absolute precision because a sloppy sentence in a legal filing is a problem for an actual person. The job sharpens an analytical mind whether you want it sharpened or not.

It also gave Mac his first real exposure to East Asia. Okinawa is not Tokyo and not Taipei, but it taught him how to live in a place that wasn't built for him — to listen more, talk less, and treat curiosity as a default setting. Those four years planted seeds for everything that came after.

Teaching

Ten-plus years in the Taiwanese classroom

After the Marines, Mike McQuillan came to Taiwan to teach — and stayed. For more than a decade he has taught English and content subjects to students from kindergarten through adult learners, with most of his work concentrated in Taichung. Mac designs his own lesson plans, his own curriculum arcs, his own assessment instruments. If a textbook works, he uses it; if it doesn't, he writes a better one.

His method, if it has a name, is something close to adventure-led mastery: every unit has a story, every story has a stake, and every lesson asks the student to do something — not just absorb something. The decision to write his own materials wasn't ideology. It was practical. The materials he wanted didn't exist, so he made them.

School Building

Two schools in Beitun, Taichung

In Beitun district, Taichung, McQuillan has played a foundational role in building two schools. At Exemplar English, he shaped the curriculum, the course structure, and the assessment frameworks — the spine that everything else hangs on. At Adventureland, he worked closer to the surface: the identity, the classroom materials, the culture in the hallways. The two roles are different jobs that use the same toolkit.

Building a school is a humbling exercise. You can have the best lesson on paper, but if a five-year-old is hungry, or scared of the door, or sitting next to a kid he just argued with, none of it works. Mac learned to design downward — start with what the student is actually feeling at 9:02 a.m., and reverse-engineer the curriculum from there. It is a lesson that has shaped both schools and, eventually, both book series.

Books

Adventures in Mr. Mac's Class

The McQuillan body of writing now runs on two parallel tracks. The first is a 12-volume storybook series — one volume per grade, from Grade 1 through Grade 12 — built around a magical watch that lifts a class out of its chairs and drops it into a different time, place, or problem. The Magic School Bus comparison is fair and Mac welcomes it. The difference is that the books are written by a working teacher who still has to face Monday morning, so the adventures land where lessons actually need to land.

The second track is a companion textbook series — same 12-grade arc — that turns each storybook adventure into something a teacher can actually run. The story of why the magical watch became the spine of the series is, like most things in McQuillan's life, a story about a problem he couldn't find an existing answer to. So he wrote the answer.
